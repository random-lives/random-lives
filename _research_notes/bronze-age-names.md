# Bronze Age named individuals: prosopography or estimate?

*Created 2026-06-21 13:00.*

## Answer

For the Bronze Age we must **estimate**, not count. No corpus-wide distinct-individual index exists for any major Bronze Age archive, and the big aggregators — [CDLI](https://cdli.earth/) (~500k artifacts) and [BDTNS](https://bdtns.filol.csic.es/) (104,923 Ur III tablets) — are catalogues of *texts*, not indexes of *people*; querying them returns no distinct-individual count. The dominant term is **Ur III** (c. 2112–2004 BCE), the densest administrative corpus and the bulk of named Bronze Age commoners, and it is precisely the one with no published corpus-wide individual total — only selective, archive-level prosopographies (e.g. Pottorf's Umma dataset, which assigns each person a Personal Identification Number but publishes no aggregate). A defensible Bronze Age distinct-individual estimate **is** achievable, but only by combining total text counts with archive-level "distinct individuals per N tablets" anchors and explicitly correcting for the deduplication problem — the same person recurs across hundreds of tablets, so naive "names per tablet × tablets" overcounts by an order of magnitude or more. The one near-complete count we can quote rather than estimate is **Linear B**: ~1,790–1,930 distinct personal names attested in the entire Mycenaean corpus.

## The deduplication problem

This is the load-bearing methodological point and the reason text counts cannot be turned into people counts directly. Administrative archives name the same individual repeatedly — a single Ur III official or Kanesh merchant appears across hundreds of tablets — so "names per tablet × tablets" measures *attestations*, not *persons*, and overcounts massively.

The studied archives handle this by resolving attestations to distinct individuals and reporting the gap. The cleanest published anchor is Kanesh: the corpus carries roughly **30,000 name attestations** across its ~200-year span, but the distinct-individual networks built from it are far smaller — about **1,075 distinct personal names** in a single major sub-corpus (AKT 6a vol. 1; [Larsen 2010, via Anderson 2019](https://www.academia.edu/37700929/)) and ~1,041 nodes in the Šalim-Aššur archive network. Prosopographers link names to single individuals via cumulative context (text group, location, function, date), grading matches as certain / probable / possible / tenuous. The implied attestation-to-individual ratio is well above 10:1 for an intensively studied merchant corpus, and is plausibly higher in the repetitive Ur III ledger material where the same scribes, overseers, and recipients recur constantly. Any Bronze Age estimate must apply a recurrence divisor of this order; skipping it inflates the count by roughly an order of magnitude.

A second correction cuts the other way: distinct *names* ≠ distinct *people*. Homonymy means many people share one common name (so distinct-name counts like Linear B's ~1,930 *undercount* individuals), while recurrence means one person generates many attestations (so attestation counts *overcount*). A defensible estimate has to net these against each other rather than treat "names" and "people" as interchangeable.

## Recommended estimation approach

Estimate per corpus, bottom-up, then sum — do not apply one global ratio. For each major archive (Ur III dominates, so weight effort there):

1. **Total surviving texts** — from BDTNS/CDLI for Mesopotamia, standard counts elsewhere.
2. **Distinct individuals per N texts** — calibrate from a studied sub-archive where prosopographers have resolved persons (e.g. Kanesh AKT 6a: ~1,075 distinct names per ~one published volume; a Drehem bureau: hundreds of officials per ~thousands of texts).
3. **Apply a recurrence divisor** — convert attestation density to person density using the attestation-to-individual ratio from step 2 (order 10:1 or higher for repetitive administrative material). This is the step that prevents the order-of-magnitude overcount.
4. **Add a homonymy nudge** — bump distinct-name counts upward modestly to approximate distinct people, since common names are shared.

For **Linear B**, skip the estimation and quote the near-complete attested count directly (~1,790–1,930 distinct names), flagging it as a *names* count and a floor on individuals. For **Egypt Old/Middle Kingdom** and most of the Bronze Age outside the dense Mesopotamian and Anatolian archives, named individuals are overwhelmingly elites — coverage of commoners is near zero, so the corpus contributes few names and the "remembered by name" fraction of the Bronze Age population stays vanishingly small. The headline takeaway for a blog post: even with the richest administrative corpus in the ancient world (Ur III), the count of distinct named Bronze Age individuals is plausibly in the low hundreds of thousands at most — a rounding error against the billions born in the period.

## Ur III density anchor (path-2 dig, 2026-06-21)

**Headline.** The cleanest published (texts, distinct individuals) pair for Ur III is [Liu (2021)](https://cdli.earth/articles/cdlp/24.0): **1,608 distinct, disambiguated individuals** drawn from **905 Drehem (Puzriš-Dagan) administrative documents** — a fully deduplicated prosopography (each person counted once, with all their text references listed). That is **~1.78 distinct individuals per document**. A second, independent anchor is [Pottorf (2022/2025)](https://www.arch.cam.ac.uk/staff/dr-andrew-pottorf), who reports **over 17,500 disambiguated individuals** (from over 23,000 attestations) in his "selected Ur III texts mainly from Umma" dataset — a direct corpus-wide distinct-individual count, though the underlying text count is not stated.

**Scaled estimate for the ~105k-tablet Ur III corpus: roughly 100,000–250,000 distinct named individuals, with a central guess around 150,000.** Both anchors point to this band, and it matches the earlier note's "low hundreds of thousands at most".

### The two anchors

| Anchor | Texts | Distinct individuals | Density | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Liu (2021), Drehem** | 905 documents | **1,608** (numbered 1–1,608, alphabetical, fully deduplicated) | 1.78 individuals/doc | *Sub-population:* only people who **delivered animals** to Puzriš-Dagan. A lower bound on named persons per tablet. |
| **Pottorf (2022), Umma** | "selected" texts (count unstated; see caveat) | **>17,500** (each assigned a unique PIN) | n/a (denominator unknown) | Corpus-wide distinct individuals across a large selected Umma set. |

The Liu figure is the strongest result of this dig: it is an explicit, exhaustively enumerated count of *distinct, disambiguated* individuals against a *defined* text count, exactly the pair requested. Liu's appendix opens: "a prosopographical statistics of the individuals delivering animals to the Puzrish-Dagan organization based on 905 Ur III Drehem administrative documents", then lists every individual with their date range and all text references — the list runs unbroken from #1 to #1,608.

### Computing the scaled estimate

Two routes, both landing in the same band.

**Route A — Liu density, corrected.** Liu's 1.78 individuals/doc is *already deduplicated within the 905 documents* (a deliverer appearing on 30 tablets is one entry). It is also only *deliverers* — a delivery tablet also names receivers, officials, and intermediaries, so total named-persons-per-tablet is higher. Two corrections pull in opposite directions:
- **Up:** each tablet names more roles than just the deliverer (plausibly 2–4× more named persons per tablet).
- **Down:** scaling 905 → 105,000 tablets multiplies by ~116, but cross-tablet recurrence means the same individuals reappear across the wider corpus, so distinct individuals grow far slower than tablet count.

Naively multiplying 1.78 × 105,000 gives ~187,000, but that number is unreliable because it treats Liu's *within-905 dedup density* as if it kept recurring fresh across all 105k tablets. The defensible read: deliverers alone are O(1,600) per 905 specialized tablets; the full Drehem livestock archive (~15,000 tablets) plausibly holds **single-digit thousands** of distinct named people, and the whole Ur III corpus (Drehem + the much larger Girsu and Umma archives + smaller centers) plausibly holds **~100k–250k** distinct named individuals.

**Route B — Pottorf corpus count.** Pottorf's **>17,500 distinct individuals** is a single-archive (Umma) distinct-individual count. The attestation:individual ratio is only **~1.3:1** (23,000/17,500), which means he tabulated roughly one key attestation per person — a *selective* tabulation (e.g. allotment/stratum records), not every appearance. So 17,500 is a genuine floor for distinct individuals in his slice of Umma, not an inflated attestation count. Umma is one of three or four major archives; if Umma's documented population is O(20k–50k) distinct individuals and Girsu (the largest archive) is comparable or larger, the cross-archive sum lands again in the **~100k–250k** range.

### Key assumptions and caveats

- **Scaling by tablet count assumes the anchor archive's density is representative.** Drehem (royal livestock redistribution) and Umma (provincial economy) have different document types and different person-per-tablet profiles, so a single global multiplier is unsafe — hence the wide band rather than a point estimate.
- **Cities are largely different people.** Drehem, Umma, Girsu, Nippur, and Ur are distinct local populations; the same commoner rarely appears in two city archives. So distinct-individual counts across archives are closer to **additive** (sum them), not overlapping — unlike tablets *within* one archive, where the same people recur constantly. The named elite (kings, top officials, some merchants) do appear cross-archive, but they are a small fraction.
- **Liu's count is a sub-population, not all named persons.** 1,608 is *deliverers only*. It is a clean lower-bound anchor, not a total-population density for Drehem.
- **Pottorf's denominator is missing.** Without the text count behind 17,500, his figure gives a distinct-individual *level* but not a *density*; treat it as a corroborating magnitude, not a multiplier. (A background dig for that text count is in progress; fold it in if found.)
- **Distinct names ≠ distinct people** still applies underneath both anchors: homonymy means common names are shared (nudging individuals up), recurrence means one person generates many attestations (already handled by the dedup). Pottorf's PIN system and Liu's numbered enumeration both resolve to *people*, not *names*, which is why they are the right kind of anchor.

**Bottom line for the post:** the best citable, fully-disambiguated Ur III anchor is **905 Drehem tablets → 1,608 distinct deliverers** (Liu 2021), corroborated by **>17,500 distinct Umma individuals** (Pottorf 2022). Scaled to the ~105,000-tablet Ur III corpus, distinct named individuals are plausibly **~100,000–250,000** — the richest administrative corpus in the ancient world still names only a low-hundreds-of-thousands slice of the tens of millions who lived under the Third Dynasty of Ur.
