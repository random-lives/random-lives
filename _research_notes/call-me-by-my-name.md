## TL;DR

The post asks one question: for how many of all the humans who have ever lived do we have at least their name — a personal name recorded somewhere, whether or not anything else survives? It is told chronologically, forward from the beginning, and the analytical payoff is locating the point where counting named individuals stops making sense. For roughly the first 95% of human existence (c. 200,000 BCE to c. 3400 BCE) there are zero personal names: tens of billions of people born, not one name. The first named individuals appear with the earliest writing — most likely "Kushim" on Uruk accounting tablets c. 3400–3000 BCE, though that may be an institution rather than a person. From there names accumulate, region by region and database by database, but stay a tiny sliver of the population: a global pre-1500 total of roughly 2 to 4 million named individuals out of ~60 billion born, about 1 in 15,000 to 1 in 40,000. The central thesis is that the question runs through two regimes — an **enumeration regime**, where so few people are named that we can count them in databases, and a **coverage regime**, where so many are named via parish and civil registers that the question flips to "what fraction were *not* recorded?" The crossover is not a single date; it is geographically staggered across roughly 1600 (Catholic Europe) to 1950 (most of the world) and beyond (Sub-Saharan Africa, still 51% today). The birth-weighted "ever-named" fraction probably crossed 50% only in the late 20th century, because births skewed to Asia and Africa where registration lagged Europe by decades.

## 1. Prehistory: nothing

For about the first 95% of human existence there is not a single personal name. Anatomically modern humans go back roughly 200,000 years; writing capable of recording a name appears around 3400–3200 BCE. Everything before that — every hunter-gatherer, every early farmer, every person born in the 196,000-odd years between the emergence of our species and the first scratch of proto-cuneiform — is anonymous, and will stay anonymous forever.

The scale of the silence is the thing to establish here. Depending on how you count the deep Paleolithic, somewhere on the order of tens of billions of people lived and died in this stretch. [TO COMPUTE: pre-3400 BCE births from the project's `unified_births` series — the headline "X billion people, zero names" number. The "How many people have ever lived?" post puts the all-time total near 117 billion; the pre-writing share is the relevant figure here.] Not one of them left a name. We have their tools, their bones, their hearths, their footprints, their DNA in us — the bottom tier of the pyramid, physical trace, is reasonably populated for this period. But the name tier is empty. A Mesolithic skeleton with a healed fracture and a worn molar tells us a person lived; it does not tell us what anyone called them.

This is the cleanest part of the answer and worth stating flatly: for the overwhelming majority of the human story, the probability that a randomly sampled person's name survives is exactly zero. The interesting question is entirely about the thin recent sliver, and the rest of the post is about when, where, and how fast that sliver thickens.

## 2. The first named individuals appear with the first writing

The earliest names we have are not heroes or kings. They are accountants and the people accountants kept track of.

**The leading candidate is "Kushim", c. 3400–3000 BCE.** The name appears on proto-cuneiform clay tablets from Uruk, in southern Mesopotamia, recording transactions in barley. One much-quoted tablet tallies a large quantity of barley received over 37 months, with the sign-group KUŠIM attached. Of the ~5,000 proto-cuneiform tablets excavated at Uruk, roughly 18 bear this name, usually in connection with barley accounting, which is part of why it is taken to denote a specific recurring individual — plausibly an official responsible for a brewery or storehouse ([Kushim — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushim_(Uruk_period))). The crucial caveat, which the post must state rather than bury: we cannot be sure KUŠIM is a person at all. The signs KU and ŠIM carry little context, and the combination could denote a person, an office held by successive people, or an institution. So "the first named human" is better phrased as "the first personal-name-shaped mark we can read", with an honest flag that it may be a job title.

**Gal-Sal and his two slaves, c. 3100 BCE.** A slightly later Uruk-period tablet records a slave-owner named Gal-Sal together with two enslaved people under him, a man and a woman often transliterated En-pap X and Sukkalgir ([List of oldest documents — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_oldest_documents)). If Kushim is contested, this is sometimes offered as the earliest set of names that are unambiguously people. It is worth dwelling on the social fact: the earliest individual humans we can name include two enslaved people, recorded not because anyone thought them important but because they were property someone needed to itemize. The name tier opens, from its first entries, as a byproduct of administration.

**Iry-Hor, c. 3200 BCE — the earliest individually named ruler.** In Egypt, serekh inscriptions on pottery from tomb B in the Umm el-Qa'ab necropolis at Abydos, plus a 2012 rock inscription at Wadi Ameyra in the Sinai, attest a predynastic king of Upper Egypt named Iry-Hor, written with the Horus falcon over a mouth sign ([Iry-Hor — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iry-Hor)). He is roughly contemporary with the early Uruk tablets and is sometimes cited as the earliest *living historical person* known by name. The contrast with Kushim is the useful one: Mesopotamia gives us a name through bookkeeping, Egypt through monumental kingship — the two channels (administration and the state's self-commemoration) that will dominate the named record for the next three thousand years.

**Distinguish "earliest name we have" from "earliest named author".** The first person known to have *written under their own name* comes much later: **Enheduanna**, high priestess of the moon god at Ur and daughter of Sargon of Akkad, active c. 2285 BCE, named as the first-person author of Sumerian hymns including the *Exaltation of Inanna* ([Enheduanna — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enheduanna)). The caveat here is real and worth one sentence: her works survive only in copies from ~1800 BCE and later, and some Assyriologists doubt she composed them. The post should keep these two milestones separate — a name in a barley ledger c. 3400 BCE, a self-identifying author c. 2285 BCE, more than a millennium apart.

The framing move for this section: the name tier does not open with the famous. It opens with grain tallies and a slave inventory. Fame comes later; the record begins with accounting.

## 3. Antiquity to the late medieval: names accumulate but stay a sliver

From c. 3000 BCE the named record grows, but unevenly, and almost always for the same reasons: a durable writing medium existed, a state or temple had a reason to keep personal-name records, and the documents happened to escape destruction. All three conditions rarely co-occur. Below I walk the timeline forward, carrying the regional database counts onto a single chronological spine. The structural point throughout: names remain a rare, countable thing — a sliver against the population — which is exactly what makes the enumeration regime possible.

### Bronze Age: cuneiform, Egypt, Linear B

Cuneiform is the deepest and most populous early record of ordinary people. Administrative tablets routinely list named scribes, farmers, herders, slaves, soldiers, and witnesses. The substrate is enormous: the **Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative (CDLI)** catalogues on the order of 500,000 artifacts (its older "~320,000" figure is stale; see [prosopography-access.md](prosopography-access.md)), out of an estimated 500,000+ tablets held worldwide, spanning c. 3350 BCE to the late first millennium BCE.

- **Ur III (c. 2100–2000 BCE)** is the first moment of true administrative density. The **Database of Neo-Sumerian Texts (BDTNS)** catalogues ~105,000 Ur III tablets (the access note records 105,187 as of May 2026), of an estimated 120,000+ surviving. No one has published a single authoritative count of distinct named individuals across Ur III, but archive studies imply a very high density: the Garshana archive (~1,500 tablets over eight years) names hundreds of workers; the Drehem archive runs to ~15,000 tablets. A rough extrapolation puts Ur III alone at **tens of thousands of distinct named individuals, plausibly 50,000–150,000**. [TO COMPUTE / TIGHTEN: no firm published total; the cuneiform corpus has no consolidated distinct-individual count — a real gap.]
- **Egypt, pharaonic period.** The Porter & Moss *Topographical Bibliography* indexes private individuals on monuments across the whole pharaonic record but publishes no total. The standout is **Deir el-Medina**, the New Kingdom royal tomb-builders' village — the most intensively documented pre-classical community anywhere, with ~113 named workmen, scribes, and foremen across the Ramesside period, plus many wives and children, for a village of only a few hundred. Egypt's big numbers come later, in the Greco-Roman papyri (below).
- **Linear B (Mycenaean Greece, c. 1400–1200 BCE).** Worth a sentence: the Linear B tablets are administrative inventories that name hundreds of individuals (officials, shepherds, workers) before the script and the palaces vanished. A small, self-contained pocket of names in an otherwise illiterate Aegean.

### Iron Age and Classical: the first large prosopographies

This is where database counts become substantial.

- **Neo-Assyrian Empire (c. 911–609 BCE).** The **Prosopography of the Neo-Assyrian Empire (PNA)** records more than 25,000 individuals from written sources; an open social-network extract catalogues over 17,000 (the two figures are distinct, not a discrepancy). Over 8,000 distinct names attested.
- **Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid Babylonia (c. 620–330 BCE).** **Prosobab** records ~21,000 persons across ~5,000 cuneiform tablets.
- **The Greek world.** The **Lexicon of Greek Personal Names (LGPN)** is the authoritative onomasticon, covering the late 8th century BCE to c. 600 CE from Marseilles to India. Its research archive holds ~400,000 individuals; the published volumes (I–VA, online) contain 300,582 individuals sharing 35,982 distinct names. Athenian sub-corpora overlap heavily: Kirchner's *Prosopographia Attica* (1901) has 15,588 entries; the "Persons of Ancient Athens" project has expanded past 100,000 entries. The underlying source is ~150,000 searchable Greek inscriptions in PHI.
- **The Roman world.** This is the largest commoner corpus of antiquity, almost entirely funerary. The **Epigraphik-Datenbank Clauss/Slaby (EDCS)** now holds ~542,000 Latin and Greek inscriptions (up from the 509,600 of 2018); the **Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum (CIL)** catalogues ~180,000–200,000. No one has rigorously counted distinct individuals, but because the corpus is dominated by tombstones naming the deceased plus family and dedicators, a reasonable guess is **400,000–700,000 distinct Roman-era individuals named in surviving Latin inscriptions**, the bulk of them ordinary freedpersons and their families rather than elites. The elite is separately well-mapped: **Prosopographia Imperii Romani (PIR)** has ~14,000–15,000 senators and equestrians (31 BCE–c. 300 CE); **Prosopography of the Later Roman Empire (PLRE)** has "over 10,000" entries (260–641 CE), overwhelmingly the governing class. [The access note flags **EDH — Epigraphic Database Heidelberg** (~81,000 inscriptions, explicitly person-modeled, fully open) as the cleanest route to a defensible Roman distinct-person count — better than guessing from EDCS.]
- **Greco-Roman Egypt.** The single largest aggregator is **Trismegistos People (TM People)**: **388,901 distinct individuals**, 568,999 attestations, 43,097 distinct names, drawn from published texts of Egypt 800 BCE–800 CE across Greek, Demotic, Coptic, Latin, Aramaic, and more. Egyptian aridity preserves papyri at a density matched nowhere else, so a large share of Greco-Roman Egypt's taxpaying adult male population is probably named somewhere in the papyrological record. (Prosopographia Ptolemaica, ~50,000 entries, is mostly folded into TM People.)

### Late Antiquity and Byzantium

Three overlapping projects cover the Byzantine millennium, biased heavily toward officials, clergy, and commanders:

- **PLRE** (above) to 641 CE.
- **Prosopography of the Byzantine Empire (PBE), 641–867** — several thousand, no published total.
- **Prosopographie der mittelbyzantinischen Zeit (PmbZ)** — ~21,500 individuals (c. 11,500 for 641–867; c. 10,000 for 867–1025).
- **Prosopography of the Byzantine World (PBW), 1025–1180s** — ~10,000 individuals, ~60,000 factoids.

Roughly **40,000 distinct named Byzantines** are preserved across 330–1204 CE in these databases (post-PLRE, so not double-counting Rome). The ordinary Byzantine peasant is essentially absent.

- **China and East Asia.** The **China Biographical Database (CBDB)** is the single largest pre-modern biographical database in the world: **649,533 individuals** as of May 2025 (up from ~427,000 in 2019). It is heavily biased toward officials and literati who appear in dynastic histories, gazetteers, and examination records. East Asia pre-1500: plausibly **500,000+**, overwhelmingly Chinese, male, and elite.
- **The Islamic world (c. 600–1500).** The **Onomasticon Arabicum** holds ~27,000–28,000 scholars and notables online (the old Caetani card archive held ~250,000 cards). The *tabaqat* biographical tradition (Ibn Sa'd's ~4,250 notices, then Ibn Hajar, al-Sakhawi, and others) covers thousands of scholars each, with heavy overlap. The remarkable exception is the **Cairo Geniza**: Goitein's index (950–1250 CE, the Mediterranean Jewish world) covers ~35,000 individuals — ordinary commoners named in their own letters, contracts, marriage agreements, and shopping lists, derived from ~400,000 manuscript fragments. Nothing else from pre-1500 gives that resolution on non-elite urban life. Regional total: order of **100,000+**, dominated by scholars, rulers, poets, and the Geniza.
- **Medieval Western Europe.** Numbers are small before the late-period megadocuments, and most of the total comes from three of them.
  - **Prosopography of Anglo-Saxon England (PASE)** — ~19,807 named individuals, c. 597–1100.
  - **Domesday Book (1086)** — counts 268,984 heads of household but *names* only a small fraction: ~2,500 continental persons plus a few hundred (low thousands of) English land-holders and tenants-in-chief. Humbler villeins were counted by category, not named. England in 1086 had ~1.5–2 million people, so Domesday names something like 5% of the living population.
  - **People of Medieval Scotland (1093–1371)** — ~21,000 individuals from ~8,600 documents.
  - **Florentine Catasto of 1427** — ~60,000 heads of household covering **over 260,000 individuals** across Tuscany. A rare pre-1500 document that names essentially the entire regional population, including women and children. It alone contributes a quarter-million to the medieval European total.
  - Pre-1500 parish registers are scattered and rare (Gemona del Friuli 1379, Siena 1381, Florence 1428, Bologna 1459); the systematic *registres* of France and England are almost entirely post-1538.
  - The **Oxford Dictionary of National Biography** has ~60,000 articles across all periods, of which the pre-1500 share is **~5,000 "medieval lives"** (≈9–10%).
  - Monastic *libri vitae* and necrologies (Fulda, St. Gall, Reichenau) preserve perhaps tens of thousands of bare commemorative names each. [TO TIGHTEN: no consolidated count of distinct names across the European *libri memoriales* — an open gap.]

  Medieval Latin Europe before 1500: **well under 1 million named**, most of it from Domesday, the Catasto, and late Italian urban sources. Before 1200, outside England and a few Carolingian and monastic sources, the numbers collapse.

- **South Asia.** No CBDB-scale prosopography exists. Names come from Ashokan edicts (very few beyond Ashoka and his officers), Buddhist donative inscriptions (Sanchi >800, Bharhut 136, plus Amaravati, Karle, Nasik — naming donors, monks, nuns, merchants), and copper-plate grants and temple inscriptions naming grantors, recipients, and witnesses. A very rough guess: **well below 100,000, maybe ~50,000** — strikingly low against India's huge share of premodern humanity. [Caveat from the access note: the live, downloadable **Siddham** and **DHARMA** epigraphic corpora mean South Asia is a narrower gap than the round-number guess implies; the ~50,000 is very uncertain and should be flagged as such.]
- **Pre-Columbian Americas.** A few thousand named individuals total in the surviving pre-contact record: ~152 Maya kings plus 4 ruling queens across 11 kingdoms in Martin and Grube's *Chronicle*, several hundred more elite Maya in inscriptions, and a few hundred rulers and nobility in Central Mexican codices. The post-conquest **Matrícula de Huexotzinco (1560)** records glyph-names for over 10,000 individuals — a rare pre-contact-style document surviving into the colonial period, but post-contact. The total is vastly smaller than any other civilizational area, reflecting late and narrow writing systems plus active colonial destruction; khipu remains largely undecoded.
- **Other regions.** Aramaic Hatra (376 names); the Hebrew Bible (~1,940–3,000 named, of whom ~53 independently verified archaeologically); Safaitic, Thamudic, and Hismaic Arabian graffiti — tens of thousands of "X son of Y was here" rock inscriptions catalogued by OCIANA (now at Ohio State; the old Oxford URL is dead), distinct-individual count unpublished but plausibly large enough to move the global total; Sogdian (1,600+ names); and Sub-Saharan Africa (Aksumite Ge'ez, Nubian Meroitic and Old Nubian, post-1300 Timbuktu manuscripts), a few thousand named from indigenous sources before 1500.

### Who gets named — the hierarchy that holds across the whole timeline

The qualitative regularity is constant from Uruk to the Catasto. Rank-ordered by how routinely names enter the record:

1. **Rulers and their families** — named almost by definition (Iry-Hor, the dynastic histories, monumental inscriptions).
2. **State administrators, officials, military officers** — the bulk of PNA, PIR, PLRE, the Byzantine projects, PASE, CBDB.
3. **Clergy and religious scholars** — the *tabaqat* tradition, ODNB medieval entries, Buddhist donor inscriptions.
4. **Literate professionals: scribes, poets, doctors, jurists** — attested as authors and routine document-signers.
5. **Tax-paying heads of household and landowners** — named in censuses (Domesday, Catasto, Matrícula, Chinese *qingce*), but usually only the head; women and children reduced to a count.
6. **Commoners in routine administration** — large numbers (the Egyptian papyri, Mesopotamian tablets, Attic stelai) because someone had to record who got a ration or signed a contract.
7. **Slaves, conscripts, dependents** — named disproportionately in legal contexts (manumissions, sales, runaway notices), often without patronym or status. An under-appreciated bulk source — and, in Gal-Sal's tablet, the very first entries in the record.
8. **Women** — universally under-represented, with male-to-female attestation ratios typically between 3:1 and 10:1. Exceptions: Geniza *ketubbot*, elite Roman and Egyptian funerary inscriptions, and the Tuscan *catasti*, which name every household member.

A rough rule explains the unevenness: named preservation is highest where (a) a durable medium existed (clay, stone, papyrus-in-dry-climate), (b) a state or temple had reason to keep name records, and (c) the documents escaped destruction. Mesopotamia got all three for ~2,000 years, so a *greater* fraction of ordinary Mesopotamians may be named in surviving records than of ordinary medieval Germans. Medieval Europe fails (c) badly; India fails (a) — palm-leaf and bark rarely survive; pre-Columbian America fails (a) and was actively destroyed.

## 4. The transition: from enumeration to coverage

This is the analytical core of the post. At some point, counting named individuals one database at a time stops working — not because the databases run out, but because names stop being a rare countable sliver and become the norm. The clean way to frame this is two regimes:

- **Enumeration regime.** So few people are named that the named individuals are themselves the countable object. You build a prosopography, you add up the entries, you reason about overlap. The numerator (people named) is small and the interesting quantity; the denominator (people born) is vast and roughly fixed. Everything in Sections 2 and 3 is the enumeration regime. The output is "2 to 4 million names out of 60 billion".
- **Coverage regime.** So many people are named — via parish registers, then civil registration — that counting the named individuals is hopeless and pointless. The question inverts: not "how many were named?" but "what fraction were *not* recorded?" The interesting quantity becomes the *gap* — the unregistered remainder — and it is small and shrinking. The output is "77% of births registered, 150 million children missing".

The crossover is the moment a region's records flip from naming a countable elite-plus-fragments to naming substantially everyone. It is not a single global date. It is geographically staggered, and that staggering is the whole story:

- **Catholic and Protestant Europe, c. 1600.** Parish registration is the hinge. France's Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539) required baptismal records; the Ordinance of Blois (1579) added marriages and burials; the Council of Trent (1563) made baptism and marriage registers a universal Catholic requirement, with death registers following in 1614; England's Cromwellian injunctions of 5 September 1538 required every parish to keep registers. Coverage lagged the mandates by a century or more — surviving English registers thin before 1597, Scottish rural parishes often have nothing before the early 1800s, non-conformists are missing throughout — but by ~1600 the core areas of England, France, and Italy are plausibly past 50% of births recorded by personal name, the first large populations to cross into the coverage regime.
- **Tokugawa Japan, c. 1670–1872 — the pre-modern outlier.** The *shūmon aratame-chō* ("religious investigation registers"), born of anti-Christian surveillance after the Shimabara Rebellion and extended nationwide with the *terauke* system by 1664, recorded the name, age, and relationship of *every* household member, annually, for about two centuries. This is the clearest case of universal pre-modern individual-name registration anywhere — Japan enters the coverage regime a century before most of Europe completes it, then formalizes it with the Meiji *koseki* (1872).
- **Most of the world, c. 1850–1950.** Civil registration — state-run, secular, legally mandated, naming every individual — spreads out of Revolutionary France (the *état civil* of 1792, with mayors replacing priests from 1793) as a Napoleonic transplant through the Low Countries and southern Italy in the 1790s–1810s, is adopted independently by the UK and newly unified states in the 1830s–1870s (England and Wales 1837, mandatory 1875; Scotland 1855; Italy 1866; Spain 1871; the German Empire 1876), and arrives latest in Russia (secular ZAGS, 1917). The US had no federal system; states joined a national birth-registration area between 1915 and 1933, with half to three-quarters of late-19th-century births going unregistered before then, especially in the rural South and for African Americans. Latin America ran on parish registers from the 1530s and layered civil registration on top unevenly through the 19th century (Brazil 1850, Argentina 1886, Mexico 1859–61, Bolivia not until 1940). Most of these populations cross into the coverage regime somewhere between 1850 and 1950.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa, still incomplete.** Civil-registration laws mostly arrived in the 1960s–70s, after independence, and implementation has stalled. As of 2024 the regional birth-registration rate is **51%** and essentially flat — it "has not improved over the last 16 years". Parts of the continent have not yet entered the coverage regime at all.

This directly engages, and confirms, Damon's stated intuition: **"the awkward period is probably somewhere between 1500 and 1900."** That is right, and the two-regimes frame sharpens *why* it is awkward and *why* it is a period rather than a date. It is awkward precisely because it is the band over which different regions cross the regime boundary — Catholic Europe near the early end (~1600), the bulk of the world near the late end (~1850–1950), with the global picture a smear across the whole interval rather than a step. Within 1500–1900 neither method works cleanly for the world as a whole: too many people for enumeration, too few regions in coverage. If anything the band should be widened slightly at the back end — Sub-Saharan Africa pushes the "not yet fully in coverage" frontier past 1900 and into the present.

## 5. The modern era: the coverage view, and living memory

In the coverage regime the natural unit is the unregistered remainder, and the modern numbers are well measured.

**Civil registration spreads and coverage rises.** The regional timeline for crossing the 50% and 95%-of-births thresholds, name-recorded in a surviving record (treating a head-of-household register as naming a child if they later appear as such a head — an imperfect proxy):

| Region | >50% of births name-recorded | >95% of births name-recorded |
|---|---|---|
| Core Catholic Europe (France, Italy, Iberia) | c. 1600–1650 (parish) | c. 1870–1900 |
| England/Wales | c. 1600 (parish, patchy) | c. 1880 (post-1875 compulsion) |
| Scotland | c. 1700 (OPR, uneven rural) | c. 1870 (post-1855 civil) |
| Scandinavia | c. 1700 (Lutheran parish); Sweden effectively universal from 1749 | c. 1800 |
| Germany (Prussia) | c. 1650 (parish) | c. 1880 (Standesamt) |
| Russia | c. 1800 (Orthodox parish), rural lag | c. 1930 (Soviet ZAGS) |
| United States | c. 1880 (New England earlier) | c. 1940 (post-registration-area) |
| Latin America (settled/Catholic) | c. 1650 (parish; lower for indigenous) | c. 1950 |
| China | c. 200 BCE for heads of household | c. 1990 (PRC hukou near-universal) |
| Japan | c. 1700 (shūmon aratame-chō, ~universal) | c. 1880 (koseki) |
| Korea | c. 1700 (hojeok, status-biased) | c. 1920 (colonial koseki extension) |
| Ottoman core | c. 1550 heads of household (tahrir); individuals c. 1880 | c. 1930 (Republican Turkey) |
| India / South Asia | c. 2000 | c. 2020 (approaching) |
| Sub-Saharan Africa | **not yet** (~51% in 2024) | **not yet** |

Read vertically, crossing 50% globally is a roughly 400-year transition from ~1600 to ~2020; crossing 95% is compressed into the last ~150 years and still incomplete for a meaningful fraction of today's births. (For pre-modern head-of-household systems, the proxy matters: Han China's census of 2 CE returned 57.7 million people in 12.4 million households, the Ming Yellow Registers recompiled every decade, the Ottoman *tahrir* and Joseon *hojeok* all named heads, not everyone — so "heads of household ≠ individuals" caps even the best pre-modern systems at naming maybe ~20% of living individuals.)

**The present.** UNICEF's *Right Start in Life, 2024 update* puts under-5 birth registration at **77% worldwide** (up from 75% in 2019): 95% in Latin America and the Caribbean, 94% in Eastern and South-Eastern Asia, 78% in Central and Southern Asia (the fastest-improving region, up from under 40% in the early 2000s), and 51% in Sub-Saharan Africa. Roughly **150 million** children under 5 are unregistered, with a third in just three countries (DRC, Ethiopia, Tanzania); a further ~50 million are registered but have no certificate. At today's birth rates that is on the order of ~30 million babies a year whose names are recorded nowhere at the state level. The coverage regime is real but not yet universal — the question still has a numerator-and-denominator answer at the edges.

**Living memory is a separate, parallel channel.** Everyone alive knows their own name. People who died within living memory are still named by someone who knew them — a hard ceiling of roughly 100 years, reaching back to people born in the late 1800s. Before that it is purely a record-keeping question: a great-great-grandmother's name survives only because it was written in a parish register or a family Bible, which is to say it is really a coverage-regime fact wearing the costume of memory. The two channels converge in the present (everyone living is both remembered and registered) and diverge fast going back, with the written record outrunning living memory before ~1920.

## 6. Synthesis: the shape of the answer

The chronological walk reveals the shape before it reveals the number. The curve of "fraction of births whose name survives" is flat at zero for ~95% of human history, lifts almost imperceptibly with the first writing (still ~1 in 15,000 to 1 in 40,000 across all of pre-1500), and then bends sharply upward over a narrow recent band as one region after another crosses from enumeration into coverage — Europe around 1600, most of the world between 1850 and 1950, parts of Africa not yet. It is a hockey stick whose blade is the last few centuries, and whose handle is essentially the entire human story.

I am deliberately *not* forcing a single computed probability here — Damon has not asked to compute the birth-weighted ever-named fraction yet, and it would be false precision to state one. But the qualitative answer is firm enough to state plainly: **the birth-weighted ever-named fraction probably crossed 50% only in the late 20th century.** The reason is the interaction of two facts established above — births in the 19th and 20th centuries skewed heavily toward Asia and Africa, exactly the regions where registration lagged Europe by decades, so the global weighted average is dragged well below what Europe's early start would suggest. A Swede born in 1800 is named with near-certainty; a person born in Sub-Saharan Africa in 1900 has close to zero chance of appearing by name in any surviving record; and there are vastly more of the latter. The 50% crossing waits on the coverage regime reaching the populous, late-registering world, which is a 20th-century event.

Two numbers in this post are now computable rather than guessable, and the post should compute them rather than round-number them:

1. **The CBDB pre-1500 count.** The note's "200,000–400,000" for China is a guess; CBDB ships as a single downloadable SQLite file, and the pre-1500 subset can be derived exactly by querying the persons table on death/index year `< 1500` or by dynasty code. This is the most valuable single computation, and it tightens the largest uncertain entry in the global pre-1500 table. See [prosopography-access.md](prosopography-access.md) for the download target and method. [TO COMPUTE]
2. **The birth-weighted ever-named fraction (and its curve over time).** Combine the project's `unified_births` series (births by year and region, already computed for "How many people have ever lived?") with the regional 50%/95% coverage timeline in Section 5 to produce a birth-weighted estimate of the fraction of all humans ever named — and, more usefully than a single scalar, the fraction-named-by-birth-year curve, which is the real answer. This is the open quantitative question the post is built around. [TO COMPUTE]

A closing note on what the framing buys us: the reason no one can just "count the named" and divide by 117 billion is that the two regimes never coexist in a single countable form. For the enumeration era the numerator is small and knowable and the answer is "almost no one". For the coverage era the numerator is uncountable but the *complement* is small and knowable, and the answer is "almost everyone, minus a measurable gap". The post's job is to walk the reader from the first regime to the second and show that the boundary between them — not any single date or any single number — is the answer to where counting names stops making sense.
